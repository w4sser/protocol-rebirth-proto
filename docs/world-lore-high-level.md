# Protocol Rebirth — High-Level Lore & Narrative Bible

**Working narrative canon v0.4 — 17 November 2026**  
**Owner:** Robert / Wild Labs  
**Purpose:** A shared high-level story foundation for design, writing, art, audio, production and game implementation.  
**Approval boundary:** The tone, pillars, BIT's eventual earned-trust relationship arc, unnamed home-country rule, anti-submarine intelligence origin, progression from impossible observation and information reconstruction to civilization continuity (A to C), deliberate known-risk experiment, and **recognizable world history as the player's discovery/anticipation engine** are **owner-approved direction**. Exact historical scenes and mechanics are proposals until selected. Exact physics, consequences, character identities and ultimate answers remain **open**; protect late-game reveals in a private writing source.

> An endless world to explore. A mystery waiting to be understood. And a little companion who never stopped waiting for you.

## 1. The promise

Protocol Rebirth is a short-session, PvE extraction shooter set in a hauntingly beautiful unnamed northern archipelago. The Operator risks equipment and recovered supplies in dangerous expeditions, restores a ruined coastal bunker and gradually opens a vast world of abandoned military infrastructure, intimate human traces and unresolved mysteries.

Its narrative gives players **three independent reasons to return**:

1. **Survival and progress:** Recover useful objects, restore rooms, grow stronger and reach dangerous places.
2. **Curiosity and discovery:** What happened here? Why do old systems continue working? What is waiting beyond the islands?
3. **Connection and hope:** BIT remembers the Operator, has waited for them and becomes a companion worth returning to.

The third reason matters: players are not only returning to an activity or progression system, but to someone.

**Tone:** Dark, but never hopeless. Beauty is integral to the story, not relief from it.

## 3. The world

**The bunker — Home, recovery and reconnection.** It begins damaged, unpowered and quiet. The first restored room changes the feeling of home. The bunker is also where the Operator first meets BIT, whose small practical presence makes the space feel inhabited. Light, repaired machines, sound and BIT's movement provide visible progress. The original first raid happens **before** that meeting, consistent with the locked FTUE.

**The archipelago — Wonder, risk and freedom.** Beyond the bunker lie windswept pines, rounded granite, narrow passages, distant islands and open sea under a calm, beautiful northern summer night. Its vastness is deliberately revealed through cinematic framing. A distant lighthouse becomes an emotional landmark before it becomes an objective.

**The town and deeper installations — Lives interrupted and hidden purpose.** An abandoned settlement shows evidence of ordinary people; more restricted facilities reveal what researchers and military operators tried to understand or contain. Danger comes from autonomous systems and the consequences of long-ago decisions, not necessarily a simple present-day evil mastermind.

The world is not a static quest board. A distant structure can wake, a signal can return, or an old route can change while the Operator is elsewhere. Meaningful events leave visible, persistent consequences.

## 4. Historical spine — owner-approved intent, open mechanisms

The institutions' motivation and emotional trajectory are agreed; the literal truth of the phenomenon and the Incident is not.

1. **Military intelligence.** A coastal hydrophone network is deployed to detect suspected enemy submarines. The first researchers want better surveillance: intercept communications, decode unfamiliar signals, understand an opponent's movements and intentions.
2. **The impossible observation.** Analysis reveals more than military intelligence. The same phenomenon appears to let investigators **see or reconstruct events no available sensor could have observed** — potentially inaccessible places or moments from the distant past. The first undeniable demonstration must be grounded in a concrete, verifiable detail; do not rely solely on characters announcing that it is impossible.
3. **A — reconstruction of lost information.** The classified project shifts from listening to opponents to recovering information presumed gone forever. Scientists begin asking whether recorded history and human knowledge could be reconstructed. They do **not yet know** whether these are genuine views of the past, extraordinary inference, fragments of memory or something else.
4. **C — civilization continuity.** The wider possibility transforms the mission: preserve knowledge, cultural records, critical systems and the capacity to restore civilization after collapse. The scope of ambition is what changes; resurrection of specific individuals (B) is not the program's official intermediate goal.
5. **The deliberate experiment.** The team chooses to attempt an active experiment with the phenomenon, despite recognizing it is dangerous. They believe the hazard is sufficiently manageable, but fail to predict the **nature and scale** of what follows. The experiment triggers the Incident; catastrophe is not what they intended.
6. **The aftermath.** The region is radically altered and depopulated, while fragments of infrastructure persist. The precise mechanism, extent, timeline and human fate remain writer-private open decisions.
7. **The return.** The Operator eventually returns. Damaged BIT recognizes them after the first raid, but his testimony becomes less secure as the Operator finds evidence of what happened.

### The impossible observation — narrative design boundary

We want the player to feel the disturbing wonder of witnessing a real human moment from a time or place that nobody should have been able to see. A mundane, verifiable detail is stronger than a vague paranormal image: for example, a recovered image shows someone hiding an object in a room before surveillance existed; the object can later be found in that place. **This is an illustrative story device, not an approved specific scene or proof of time travel.**

Separate three things throughout the story:

- **What the instruments visibly produce:** the phenomenon can apparently reconstruct inaccessible human events, not merely intercept current audio.
- **What the researchers believe:** they are observing true events and can therefore recover knowledge that should be lost.
- **What the audience can finally prove:** left open until the private World Truth is decided. The signal may be genuine history, an imperfect reconstruction or a more unsettling mechanism.

**Important boundary:** Technological causality can become intelligible without settling existential questions about consciousness, loss and connection. Nothing here establishes that people can be resurrected. Do not expose the country's real identity in any evidence or dialogue.

### Historical Echoes — recognition, anticipation, revelation

**Owner-approved creative principle:** The player should recognize familiar real historical periods and events *before the game names them*. Their existing knowledge creates anticipation and the satisfying feeling of having worked out the reveal. Think of a recognizable next era as a landmark to look forward to, **not a history lesson or quiz**.

**Three-stage story grammar:**

1. **Recognition:** A partial, in-world signal reconstruction shows iconic, historically accurate clues — material, architecture, sounds, clothing, engineering and practices — without captions naming the period. Players who know the subject may identify it immediately; others still see an intriguing image.
2. **Anticipation:** A following fragment increases specificity, showing a landmark or the setup to an event. The player begins looking forward to witnessing it. A visually legible archive preview may suggest which era can be reconstructed next, but must not become a timed reward, progress currency or forced collection checklist.
3. **Revelation:** A short, authored cinematic finally confirms the player's guess and delivers an emotionally striking view of an event or place they already know. Then it reveals a **new narrative question**: how could the early researchers have observed this, and what did they actually learn?

**Candidate historical anchors — not locked production scenes:** Egyptian pyramid construction; Roman arches, legions, aqueducts and a fully realized Colosseum at the opening of its games (80 CE); the first human Moon landing (1969); the fall of the Berlin Wall (1989). All scenes require historical review, period-consistent art, and respectful portrayal. Do not collapse mutually incompatible centuries into one composite event. Widely recognizable historical references from different parts of the world are preferable to a narrow, exclusively European canon. Major religious or contested history requires particularly careful handling; familiar icons should never be used merely as shock value.

**Illustrative pilot: Rome, 80 CE.** The first recovered recording reveals an arch-lined road and a legion standard. A later reconstruction adds arena sounds, gladiatorial equipment and glimpses of an enormous elliptical building. Only on the third beat does the camera reveal the Colosseum in use. A final detail of the observation suggests that no ordinary witness or contemporary instrument could have recorded this view. The precise "impossible" evidence and how it is verified must be authored, not hand-waved. This sequence is a **pitch**, not approved shipped content.

**Why these glimpses exist:** They are outputs from the old observation/reconstruction research, found via the Operator's present-day raids and restored bunker equipment. **They do not imply that the player physically travels in time or fights historical soldiers.** The combat world remains the unnamed coastal setting. Every major historic reveal must advance the mystery about the research, the signal, and eventually the Incident, rather than becoming an unrelated museum montage.

**Make players feel clever without excluding them:** Let clues precede labels, allow optional contextual details after the payoff, never require outside historical knowledge to progress, and do not have BIT immediately explain the reveal. The narrative beats can be viewed on mute and replayed without resetting earned progress.

### Unnamed-country rule — owner-locked

**Never identify the country of the playable bunker/archipelago within the fiction.** Place and history should feel specific through natural landscapes, coastal-defense installations, hydrophone systems, old technology and local architecture, but no script, collectible, map, UI label, sign, national symbol, fictional news report or narrative marketing copy may explicitly name or conclusively identify **that country**. This does not prohibit naming other historical places such as Rome or events elsewhere in the world when the research technology reconstructs them. Avoid exact real-world geographic coordinates, recognizable unit insignia, official agency names and flags.

This does not mean relocating the story to a named fictional nation. The landscape remains unmistakably grounded, while the national label stays absent. Art reference documents may discuss regional influences, but shipped narrative content must not.

## 5. Mystery hierarchy

| Layer | Question | Reveal policy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Immediate** | What is safe, useful and reachable? | Answer through gameplay, clear feedback and physical space; never cryptic UX |
| **Local** | Why is the lighthouse active? Who powered the radar? | Provide concrete, satisfying answers to individual mysteries |
| **Historical** | What was Protocol Rebirth, and why did the Incident happen? | Writer-owned internal truth; reveal through multiple discoveries across regions |
| **Personal** | Who was Operator to BIT? Why did BIT wait? What can BIT remember? | Emotional reveals paced across raids, home moments and restored capability |
| **Existential** | Are memories and relationships connected in a way science cannot explain? | Preserve genuine interpretive room even after the technological plot becomes intelligible |

**Do not replace real answers with endless teasing.** A mystery must reward investigation. The answer may open a deeper question, but it must also resolve something the player actually wondered about.

## 6. BIT and Operator

**BIT is the emotional anchor**, not a cute quest dispenser. He is an old, damaged CRT companion: loyal, occasionally wry, worried about the Operator, with uneven memories and limits. He waited a very long time without a fully reliable account of why.

- At home, BIT is present in the bunker between raids; his presence, movement and reactions matter beyond dialogue.
- In the field, BIT can guide, warn, distract and gradually remember; do not make him narrate every moment of silence.
- BIT introduces new gameplay systems before those systems appear, following the existing systems-first FTUE design and shared narrator components.
- His **memories** are discoverable story, not a currency purchase, a second mandatory quest track or a guaranteed reward for buying an upgrade.
- BIT may be mistaken about *facts* without lying about *feelings*. His relationship with Operator must remain emotionally legible.
- The Operator is an adult, hooded and visually anonymous character. Do not reveal the face in cinematics or casually settle personal identity.

BIT's face and body are controlled by the approved visual master and approved narrator artwork; expressive animation uses restrained body motion and established visual assets, not novel kawaii facial geometry.

### BIT and Operator — earned friendship through a rupture

**Relationship promise:** BIT is the Operator's protector, but protection and trust are not synonymous. His **intentions**, **memories** and **instructions/limitations** are three separate things. Players may trust his care before they trust his facts. An unreliable recollection can put the Operator in danger even if BIT never intended harm.

| Phase | Relationship beat | Player feeling |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Reunion** | Damaged BIT remembers and cares about the returning Operator; he helps in small, consistent ways | "Someone waited for me" |
| **2. Dependence** | Guidance and mutual survival build a bond. BIT is useful, vulnerable and more than a tutorial voice | "I need him, and he needs me" |
| **3. Contradictions** | A confident BIT memory conflicts with physical evidence or an impossible recording. He can be sincerely wrong | "What else has he got wrong?" |
| **4. Rupture** | An action or withheld truth meant to protect the Operator leads to a serious breach of trust. Both have understandable reasons, not a manufactured villain turn | "Can I afford to believe him?" |
| **5. Forced cooperation** | To reach a shared, tangible objective, they must act together despite unresolved mistrust; each can do what the other cannot | "I have to rely on him even now" |
| **6. Earned choice** | BIT accepts uncertainty, discloses what he does not know and chooses to support the Operator's informed decision even when it conflicts with a protective impulse | "He may be wrong, but he won't take my choice away" |

**Long-term story arc, not an early pilot event:** The near-breakup and forced-cooperation scene belongs in later narrative development, after the player genuinely relies on BIT. Seed subtle, fair memory contradictions earlier; save the serious rupture and reconciliation until there is enough shared history for it to matter.

**The conflict must be dramatized, not explained in a monologue.** Example direction only: BIT confidently remembers that a sealed route is safe; evidence points elsewhere, and the Operator realizes BIT has concealed or misinterpreted something consequential. A later joint action proves he will help even when he cannot guarantee safety. The precise betrayal, identity of the memory and cause of its unreliability are **not yet canon**.

**Design guardrails:** No compulsory loss of valuable gear, untelegraphed death, unfair raid failure or permanent withdrawal of core BIT abilities merely to manufacture drama. Show the rupture in safe scenes and consequential but fair gameplay choices. Preserve player agency: the Operator may distrust BIT and still choose to collaborate. Successful resolution earns **trust in honesty and intent**, not an omniscient, error-free companion. The relationship may continue evolving after the first reconciliation.


## 7. Themes and interpretation

**Loss without nihilism.** The world is damaged, but not dead. Meaning survives in details: an old household, a familiar voice, a light still maintained by nobody, or BIT waiting at home.

**Connection after separation.** Events may evoke the feeling that relationships persist beyond absence, perhaps even beyond death. This is emotional material, **not proof of a cosmic mechanism**. Science-fiction explanations and a genuinely unexplained remainder should be able to coexist.

**Hope without certainty.** There are things the Operator may never know. That does not make the effort to understand, protect or rebuild meaningless.

**Mystery with restraint.** Let players be uncertain about metaphysics, not confused about controls, requirements, prices, extraction or cause-and-effect that the gameplay needs to communicate.

Avoid: "everyone was dead all along", "it was only a dream", a simplistic simulation twist, a single preached theology, or a lore dump that prematurely resolves the mystery.

## 8. How the story reaches the player

1. **Environmental narrative:** Layouts, traces of ordinary life, military remnants, distant landmarks, changes in light and sound. No explanation required.
2. **Cinematic story beats:** Authored, full-screen, intentional departures from gameplay. The camera may reveal distant, off-screen events the Operator does not witness.
3. **BIT's presence and memory:** Genuine character moments in the bunker and the field, including current narrator cards and restoration cinematics.
4. **Discoveries:** One active clue at a time, found as part of normal raids, with optional follow-up rather than mandatory checklist objectives.
5. **World consequences:** Signals, props, environment states, access and reactions persist after meaningful events.

These layers should reinforce each other without duplicating the same exposition.

### Pacing and returning

Aim for **roughly one significant authored world/story event per week** for engaged players, with quiet weeks and additional scenes at key opening milestones. This is a creative/content cadence, not a daily task, a time-limited reward, an obligation to log in or a promise of server-synchronized happenings.

Unseen authored story events remain available until the player reaches a safe, eligible moment; missed weeks never erase story. No autoplay marathon after a long absence. A cinematic can briefly interrupt presentation, but never surprise-punish a player in active combat or extraction.

## 10. Writer's evidence contract

For each mystery, maintain five fields in the later private World Truth: **what actually happened**, **what people in the world believed**, **what evidence the player can find**, **what BIT currently thinks**, and **what remains interpretively open**. Separately track whether BIT's claims come from direct observation, damaged memory, an inference, or a protective decision. Do not make him knowingly deceptive simply because a plot twist needs it.

Every scene also needs a useful payoff: a character shift, an answer, a discovery or a persistent change. An ominous image without development is not enough. Early clues can reveal hydrophone recordings, information-reconstruction work, an expanding continuity mandate and experiment safety warnings without spoiling what the experiment ultimately did.

## 11. Decisions required before scaling content

A separate **private** writer's World Truth must settle factual causality without revealing it prematurely in the public prototype repository:

1. What first impossible event did the military intelligence researchers reconstruct, how was it verified, and how did that lead from espionage through information recovery (A) to civilizational continuity (C)?
2. What did the researchers knowingly risk in their deliberate experiment, and what unforeseen consequences actually followed?
3. What is BIT's actual origin, what causes his conflicting memories, and what specific **later** event creates the serious trust rupture?
4. What is Operator's factual relationship to BIT, the facilities and the long absence?
5. What technical truth explains the lighthouse, radio network and distant facilities?
6. Which layer remains intentionally ambiguous even to the ending?

**Public-repo caution:** This repository is public. Do not commit the complete late-game solution, unrevealed character identities, secret lines or an internal twist matrix here. Keep this document high-level; put spoiler-critical authorial truth in a private, access-controlled writing source when approved.
